# State Transition and Actualization Framework

**Status:** foundational working framework for TRT/LRT development  
**Scope:** cross-domain analysis of admissibility, transition, and actualization  
**Role:** upstream framework for cosmological origins, abiogenesis, consciousness, quantum measurement analogies, DFM initialization, and related emergence claims

## 1. Core problem

Many explanatory disputes can be normalized into a state-transition form:

\[
S_i \rightarrow S_j
\]

This notation is intentionally abstract. The relevant states may be physical, biological, informational, phenomenal, or mixed. The framework does not assume that every domain is ontologically reducible to the same type of state.

For any proposed transition from an antecedent state \(S_i\) to a consequent state \(S_j\), distinguish at least three questions.

### 1.1 Admissibility

Is \(S_j\) permitted by the governing logical and physical constraints?

Admissibility concerns possibility. A state can be admissible without being generated or actualized.

### 1.2 Transition

What mechanism, operation, or causal pathway maps \(S_i\) to \(S_j\)?

Represent this provisionally as:

\[
F(S_i)=S_j
\]

where \(F\) is a transition operator, dynamical law, mechanism, or action sufficient to produce the consequent state under specified conditions.

### 1.3 Actualization

Why does \(S_j\) actually obtain rather than merely remain one admissible possibility among alternatives?

This is the actualization question. A possibility space does not actualize itself.

## 2. TRT/LRT mapping

The framework maps naturally onto the LIA ontology.

- \(L\): logic and constraint; determines admissibility and consistency.
- \(I\): information; specifies content, structure, distinction, or state description.
- \(\mathcal{A}\): action; provides causal efficacy or transition.
- \(\chi\): actualization; the realized outcome.

Canonical relation:

\[
\chi \equiv \mathcal{A}(I\mid L)
\]

Interpretive shorthand:

> **Logic constrains. Information specifies. Action transitions. Actualization obtains.**

This relation should not be reduced to a claim that all physical transitions are conscious acts. At the physical level, lawful secondary causation may instantiate transition dynamics. The foundational question is what ultimately grounds the existence, applicability, and efficacy of those dynamics.

## 3. Emergence is not yet a transition operator

The term `emergence` is frequently used as if it were itself an explanatory mechanism:

\[
S_i \xrightarrow{\text{emergence}} S_j
\]

This is insufficient unless the term is unpacked into an independently specified transition relation or mechanism.

A rigorous emergence claim requires something of the form:

\[
F(S_i, B, C, \ldots)=S_j
\]

where the relevant boundary conditions, constraints, resources, and auxiliary assumptions are made explicit.

Working principle:

> **Naming a later state as emergent does not yet explain the transition that produces it.**

## 4. The boundary case: nothing to something

Literal nothingness is not an ordinary antecedent state.

If `nothing` means no entities, properties, laws, fields, spacetime, potentials, information, causal powers, or relations, then the expression

\[
S_{\varnothing} \xrightarrow{F} S_1
\]

is formally misleading. Both \(S_{\varnothing}\) and \(F\) already introduce structure that literal nothingness does not contain.

Therefore:

> **Nothing-to-something is not an ordinary state transition. It is the boundary case in which no antecedent physical state and no antecedent physical transition operator are available.**

This does not by itself establish a particular theology. It does expose a category mistake in explanations that treat absolute nothingness as though it were a physical state with latent transition capacity.

## 5. Cross-domain applications

The framework is intentionally general. The examples below are analogically related, not asserted to be physically identical.

### 5.1 Cosmological origins

Question:

\[
\varnothing \rightarrow S_{\text{cosmos}}
\]

The central issue is ontological origination rather than an ordinary physical transition. Any proposed naturalistic account must identify what physically exists prior to the transition and therefore has already departed from literal nothingness.

### 5.2 Abiogenesis

Question:

\[
S_{\text{prebiotic chemistry}} \rightarrow S_{\text{living system}}
\]

The explanatory burden includes more than molecular production. A proposed pathway must account for stabilization, persistence, replication, heritable variation, integration, compartmentalization, and the onset of functionally effective information-bearing relations.

This supports the related **abiogenic state-selection problem** and **pre-Darwinian stabilization problem**.

### 5.3 Brain to consciousness

Question:

\[
S_{\text{neural}} \rightarrow S_{\text{phenomenal}}
\]

A physical account may describe neural transitions, integration, reportability, and behavior. The framework asks whether those descriptions entail first-person awareness and why a particular phenomenal content is actualized.

This supports the related **conscious-state selection problem**, **phenomenal actualization problem**, and **brain-to-consciousness entailment problem**.

### 5.4 Quantum measurement and decoherence

Question, schematically:

\[
|\Psi\rangle \rightarrow S_{\text{effective classical}}
\]

The framework may use measurement/decoherence as a structural analogy for possibility, selection, stabilization, and realized state. It must not imply that abiogenesis, consciousness, or cosmological origin are literally quantum-measurement problems.

### 5.5 Designed Functional Maturity

Question:

\[
S_0 \rightarrow S_1 \rightarrow \cdots \rightarrow S_t
\]

DFM asks which features of an observed present state \(S_t\) are generated by post-initialization lawful transitions and which belong to the initialized state \(S_0\).

This is directly relevant to retrodictive age. Backward reconstruction under a lawful transition model can recover a hypothetical antecedent trajectory without independently establishing that the entire trajectory actually occurred.

## 6. State-transition sufficiency

A transition claim should be evaluated against four progressively stronger levels.

### Level 1: logical admissibility

The target state does not violate logical constraints.

### Level 2: physical permissibility

The target state is consistent with applicable physical law and resource constraints.

### Level 3: generative sufficiency

A specified mechanism and antecedent state are sufficient to generate the target state under stated conditions.

### Level 4: historical actualization

The mechanism did in fact operate from the stated antecedent condition and produced the realized target state.

These levels should not be collapsed.

Working principle:

> **Can happen, can be generated, and did happen are distinct claims.**

## 7. State selection and stabilization

Many domains contain large spaces of admissible states but comparatively narrow subsets of persistent, functional, or observed states.

Generalized question:

> **What selects, stabilizes, or privileges the realized state from the broader space of admissible alternatives?**

Possible answers differ by domain. They may include dynamical attractors, energy minima, environmental interaction, boundary conditions, control structures, semantic constraints, selection mechanisms, agency, or some combination.

The framework does not assume that one answer applies universally.

## 8. Explanation versus description

A mathematical law can accurately describe a transition relation while leaving its ontological ground unexplained.

Likewise, a probabilistic distribution can assign relative likelihoods to possible outcomes without explaining why the distribution, the possibility space, or the transition dynamics exist at all.

Therefore distinguish:

- description of state relations;
- prediction of likely transitions;
- mechanism of transition;
- grounding of the mechanism;
- actualization of a particular outcome.

These are related explanatory layers.

## 9. Research discipline

The framework should be used comparatively and symmetrically.

For any claimed transition, ask:

1. What is the antecedent state?
2. What is the consequent state?
3. What makes the consequent state admissible?
4. What transition operator or mechanism is proposed?
5. What boundary conditions and auxiliary assumptions are required?
6. Is the proposed mechanism generatively sufficient?
7. What evidence distinguishes actual history from merely possible history?
8. What explains the realized state rather than nearby alternatives?
9. Which parts of the account are direct observation, model inference, or metaphysical commitment?
10. What would count against the proposed transition account?

## 10. Canonical propositions

The following propositions are candidates for later formal refinement.

### P1. Possibility is not actuality

If \(S_j\) is admissible under \(L\), it does not follow that \(S_j\) is actual.

### P2. Admissibility is not generative sufficiency

If a transition from \(S_i\) to \(S_j\) is physically permitted, it does not follow that a known mechanism is sufficient to generate \(S_j\).

### P3. Generative sufficiency is not historical entailment

If a mechanism \(F\) can generate \(S_j\) from \(S_i\), it does not follow that \(F\) actually produced the observed \(S_j\) in history.

### P4. Emergence requires unpacking

`Emergence` does not function as an explanatory operator unless the transition relation it denotes is independently specified.

### P5. Literal nothingness supplies no physical transition resources

If literal nothingness contains no entities, properties, laws, information, potentials, or causal powers, then no ordinary physical state-transition model can begin from literal nothingness.

### P6. Actualization requires more than a possibility space

A set of admissible alternatives does not, merely by existing as a possibility structure, explain the realization of one particular member.

## 11. Relationship to Semantic Actualism

The framework aligns with the Semantic Actualism modal triplet:

1. representable;
2. logically admissible;
3. actualizable.

State-transition analysis adds explicit attention to the mechanism by which an admissible state becomes realized.

This suggests a useful distinction:

\[
\text{representable} \supseteq \text{admissible} \supseteq \text{actualizable} \supseteq \text{actualized}
\]

The inclusions are provisional and require formal care. In particular, `actualizable` should denote more than logical consistency; it should require sufficient conditions for realization under the ontology in question.
